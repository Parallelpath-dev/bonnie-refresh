02

Casual gardener interviews

We sat down with ten casual gardeners to hear how they started, where they go for help and what goes wrong. They love the idea of a garden, and every one of them has lost a plant. Most couldn’t say why.

Meet Catherine.

“Hi, I’m Cat.”

“Would you call yourself a gardener?”
“Yes.”
“Why?”

“Because I have had a garden, and I try to have a garden. Um, as often as I can.”

“My mom’s side of the family are all gardeners, so every time I would visit my grandma, she would instantly take us from the car to her garden.”

“So I always wanted to do it. I was always around it.”

“I liked the idea of having everything from scratch, and from something that you grew outside.”

Catherine, casual gardener

They want to grow. They’re afraid it’ll die.

All ten told us about a plant that didn’t make it. Most now ask AI before they ask a person, and starter plants feel like a safer bet than seed.

The ten people we heard from.

Watch or download all ten full interviews in this folder.

Main takeaways.

The patterns that kept coming up across our ten conversations.

Everyone has lost a plant.

10 of 10 told a failure story. 6 of 10 couldn’t say what went wrong.

The hard part starts after planting.

9 of 10 struggle with care, not choosing or planting. Watering comes up most.

AI is the first stop.

6 of 10 ask ChatGPT or Claude before anyone else. 4 of 10 go with whatever it says.

Gardening runs in the family.

8 of 10 learned from family, usually a parent or grandparent.

Seed isn’t about saving money.

0 of 10 chose seed because it’s cheaper. They grow from seed for the fun, the pride or the tradition.

No one named a plant brand.

0 of 10 mentioned Bonnie or any plant brand on their own.

Mystery shopping

We sent our team into stores the way a casual gardener would, with one goal: grow some food, after a history of killing plants. Here’s what they found on the shelf and in the aisle.

We shopped like beginners.

Our team visited big box stores, local garden centers and feed stores in eight markets in late October and early November. Each visit followed the same brief: ask for help, compare what’s there and note what a beginner would see.

What stood out.

The patterns that showed up from store to store.

Season decides the shelf. Bonnie was there at 3 of 6 planting-season stops and 0 of 13 off-season.

At 8 of 22 stops, there was no one to ask about plants.

What a beginner runs into.

192 zone guides, and nothing to connect you to yours.

Bonnie has a planting guide for every zone. The zone page embeds the USDA’s map to look up your zone, and from there you’re on your own.

The store finder sends people to the wrong place.

In 18 of 24 zips, results included a store that’s mislocated, doesn’t sell garden plants or is reported closed. 1,301 of its listings are Kmart. None of the 24 show stock or link to the retailer.

A store finder that knows Italy.

Typing a Colorado zip on its own pulled up Italy and Indonesia, 2 of 2 times.

Slow on a phone.

The home page scores 26 out of 100 for mobile speed, and the other pages we tested score 28 to 34. In Google’s test, the home page’s main image took 46 seconds to load.

More recipes than care.

221 of 776 articles (28%) are recipes, which the brief says to deprioritize. The top tomato guide still carries a 2011 publish date.

Sub-brands have no home.

1 of 5 has a page (Organics). An old Foodie Fresh link redirects to the home page.

Open to AI, but only for shopping.

Bonnie’s file for AI agents covers checkout, not the gardening library.

Tagged creator posts

We pulled every public post that tagged, mentioned or hashtagged Bonnie over six months, plus the creators beginners find when they search, to see who’s already talking about Bonnie and how.

Six months of posts.

Instagram, TikTok and YouTube, May 2 to Nov 2. Most posts come from everyday accounts: 85% have under 10,000 followers.

437

1.7M

What the posts tell us.

Teaching beats showing.

How-to and tip posts draw a median of 43 engagements, about 4 times the 10 for harvest updates, which make up over half of all posts.

The talk is on Instagram. Beginners learn elsewhere.

397 of 437 posts are on Instagram. YouTube and TikTok, where beginner creators rank in search, have 40 between them.

The talk follows the season.

Half the year’s posts (218) land in May and June, then drop to about 45 a month by fall.

Beginner creators don’t name plant brands.

The creators who rank for beginner questions (Epic Gardening, Gardenary, The Ripe Tomato Farms, planthack3) mentioned Bonnie, Burpee or Proven Winners in 0 of the posts captured. Several sell their own seeds or kits.

Proven Winners runs a bench. Bonnie runs one-offs.

Only 15 of the 437 posts carry a paid or gifted label, while Proven Winners tags the same two creators 6 times each.

Creators worth a call.

Some already post about Bonnie on their own. Others teach beginners and haven’t picked a plant brand. We think both could help carry the refresh.

Reviews and social

We read every Bonnie live-plant review from the past year at Home Depot, Lowe’s, Walmart.com and Amazon, plus the Reddit and Facebook threads from our search audit, and sorted what people praise and what they complain about.

A year of reviews and comments.

The plant does well in store. Online, it struggles.

In store, Bonnie averages 4.45 stars. Online, it’s 3.39. The gap is shipping. 72% of negative online reviews are about the plant’s condition when it was delivered: dead, wilted, broken or crushed in transit. In store, the complaints are about how it grew.

4×

Online reviews are four times as likely to be 1 or 2 stars: 37% vs. 9% at Home Depot and Lowe’s.

What the conversation is about.

People who grew it like it. People who priced it don’t.

In comments naming a brand, performance nets +17%, while price nets −40% and local growers −20%.

The rival in the comments is the local nursery.

105 comments name a local nursery or grower. 11 name Burpee.

Facebook is warmer than Reddit.

Among comments naming Bonnie, Facebook splits about 40% positive and 34% negative toward the brand. Reddit splits 13% and 44%, and Reddit is what AI cites most.

Price comes with a number.

147 comments quote a dollar figure.

Unsold plants in the trash is a live story.

One May 2026 Reddit post, “Straight to the trash,” drew 2,278 upvotes, and 76 comments we captured talk about plants being thrown out.
